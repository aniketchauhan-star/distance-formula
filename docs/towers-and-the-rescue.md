# Build prompt — the towers, the repair, and the rescue

Two questions and a repair between them. Screens **49 to 54**, after the
two walks — and they become the end of the game.

**Read section 0 of `docs/horizontal-distance-animation.md` first.** Then
`docs/one-more-walk.md` (the beat these follow) and
`docs/one-question-better.md` §9.3, which lists four things fixed last
build that these beats depend on.

---

## 0. What is mine to decide, and what is yours

Three things in the mocks are decisions rather than drawings. Each is one
line to overrule and nothing else depends on which way it goes.

- **The mascot.** The mocks show a boy with a tablet and a robot with a
  headset. **I have kept Swifty.** A game that changes its guide in the
  last five minutes throws away the only relationship it has built, and
  two new characters is two new art sets and two new voices. The
  *scenes* can change — that is scenery, and the leaf sweep already
  moves between them.
- **The text box and the slider.** The mock's typed box is the game's own
  combination lock; there is no reason for a third numeric control. The
  mock's **slider is worth building** — §5 says why — but as a second
  skin on the numeric question the game already has, not a new kind of
  question.
- **The backdrops.** A control room and an airport tower at dusk. If that
  art does not arrive, both beats work on the field the game already
  uses. The board is the teaching; the room is the reason to care.

## 1. What these two beats are for

### 1.1 Where the lesson has got to

By screen 48 a child has been walked through the method three times and
has done it once alone, on a pair that straddles both axes. They can do
it. What nobody knows yet is **whether they can do it when it is not
obviously the same question** — and what happens when they can't.

These two beats answer both.

### 1.2 The towers: the same method, no scaffolding at all

> Tower A at (−2, 5). Tower B at (4, −3). How long should this
> connection be?

No town, no walk, no leaves, no *"now this one"*. A question, a board,
and a box. It is the same mathematics as screen 47 and it is dressed so
that nothing about it says so — which is the only way to find out
whether what was learned was the method or the wrapper.

### 1.3 The repair: the best idea in the mocks

This is the part worth protecting. Every other wrong answer in this game
ends with the working being written out. **This one asks the learner to
substitute first**, and the mock says exactly why:

> *the learner may have made the original mistake **while substituting**,
> not while calculating.*

That is correct, and it is the single most common failure with this
formula. A child who writes `(4 − 5)² + (−3 − (−2))²` has paired x with
y; a child who writes `(4 − 2)²` has dropped a sign. Neither is an
arithmetic mistake, and a worked solution answers neither — it shows
them a correct line they will read as "yes, obviously" and then make the
same error next week.

So the repair **finds out which mistake it was** before it fixes
anything. That is the difference between feedback and teaching.

### 1.4 The rescue: the special case, and the reward

> The rescue vehicle is at (−5, −12). The control station is at (0, 0).
> How far apart are they?

One point is the origin, and that is the whole point of the beat:

```
d = √((−5 − 0)² + (−12 − 0)²) = √(5² + 12²) = √169 = 13
```

**There is nothing to take away.** Every distance in this lesson has
cost two subtractions; this one costs none, and a child who sees why has
understood what the subtraction was *for* — it was never a ritual, it
was the gap, and the gap from zero is the number itself.

And it is a **5-12-13**, after the café's 3-4-5 and the walk's 6-8-10.
Three whole answers in a row is not a coincidence a child has to be told
about, but the ones who notice have noticed something real.

### 1.5 What they can do when it ends

Find the distance between any two points on the plane, from a question
that does not announce itself, and know where their own mistake was when
they make one.

## 3. The towers  *(screen 49)*

| | where | |
|---|---|---|
| Tower A | **(−2, 5)** | second quadrant |
| Tower B | **(4, −3)** | fourth quadrant |

- across: `4 − (−2) = **6**`
- down: `−3 − 5 = **−8**`, so **8**
- `d = √(36 + 64) = √100 = **10**`

Four things those numbers are doing, all of which must survive an edit:

- **Both subtractions cross zero**, and one of them crosses it
  downwards. Screen 47 did `2 − (−4)`; this does `−3 − 5`, which is the
  other direction and the one a child is likelier to write as `−3 − 5 =
  −2`. The square forgives the sign; getting there does not.
- **The answer is whole.** 10.
- **It fits the board** with room above each marker for its mast and its
  pill, and neither marker lands on an axis number.
- **It is a 6-8-10 again** — deliberately. This is the retest of screen
  47, and a retest that changes the numbers as well as the costume
  measures two things at once and tells you neither.

**Her line.** One sentence, and it is the question:

> *"How long should this connection be?"*

Nothing before it. The beat's whole design is that it does not help.

**The hint**, one rung, in the game's own hint note:

> *"How far across? How far up?"*

The mock's hint — *"Find the distance between the two towers"* — is the
question again. A hint that restates the task is not a hint; it is a
child opening a drawer and finding it empty. This one names the first
move and stops.

**The ladder is one rung and then the repair**, not a working:

- **Wrong once**: *"Not quite. Count across, then up."* Another go.
- **Wrong twice**: the asking stops here and the repair takes over. She
  says so on the next screen, not this one — a child needs a moment to
  stop being wrong before they can start learning, which is the reason
  screens 43 and 44 are two screens and not one.

## 4. The repair  *(screens 50–53)*

### 4.1 It is not a third ask, and here is why

The rule everywhere in this game is: one nudge, then she shows it, and
**nothing is asked a third time**. The repair asks two more questions.
That is not a breach of the rule, and the difference matters enough to
write down:

A third ask is *the same question again*, and it is forbidden because
the child has already told you they cannot answer it — asking again only
teaches them that the game does not listen. The repair asks **different
questions**: not "how long is it?" but "where do the numbers go?" and
"what is 36 plus 64?" Each of those is answerable by a child who could
not answer the first, which is the whole test of whether a question is
worth asking.

And each rung of the repair is itself bounded. Miss the substitution
twice and the slots fill themselves. Miss the calculation twice and the
working is written out. Nothing in here can trap a child.

### 4.2 — *"Oops! Let's find it together."*  (screen 50)

The board does not change. The dashed line between the towers lights,
and that is all that happens.

One line, one beat, nothing to do. It exists so that the next screen is
not landing on a child who is still absorbing being wrong. Screen 43
does exactly this job in the café beat and it is the reason that beat
lands.

### 4.3 — *"Start with the Distance Formula."*  (screen 51)

The formula arrives beside the board:

```
d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)
```

Stated, not asked. They have met it; this is putting it back in front of
them, and the board it is written next to still has the two towers on it
with their coordinates showing — which is the whole reason it is written
*here* rather than in a panel of its own.

### 4.4 — *"Put the coordinates in."*  (screen 52)

The formula again, with its numbers taken out:

```
d = √((▢ − ▢)² + (▢ − ▢)²)
```

and four chips under it: **−2**, **5**, **4**, **−3**.

*(Built as the squared form, `d² = (□ − □)² + (□ − □)²` — see §11.2. The
radical will not fit the column at a slot size a child can hit, and the
squared form is the one the rest of this lesson writes anyway.)*

- **Chips, not typing.** Four text fields with minus signs in them is a
  test of a keyboard, and this screen is not about typing. Tap a chip
  then a slot, or drag it; tap a filled slot to take the chip back.
- **Each chip carries its tower's colour** — A's two in Tower A's red,
  B's two in Tower B's teal. That makes "one from each point in each
  bracket" visible without saying it, and leaves "x with x, y with y"
  to the child. Colour tells them *which tower*, never *which axis* —
  which is exactly the half of the job worth keeping.
- **Both orders are right.** `(4 − (−2))² + (−3 − 5)²` and
  `(−2 − 4)² + (5 − (−3))²` are the same number, and a build that takes
  only one of them teaches that the formula has a direction. It does
  not. Accept either, as long as the x's are together and the y's are
  together.
- **The nudge names the rule, not the answer**: *"x with x, y with y —
  across first, then up."*
- **Wrong twice**: the chips walk themselves into the slots, one at a
  time, and the beat moves on. The question is over; nothing is asked a
  third time.

This screen is the diagnosis. If the child fills it correctly first try,
their mistake was arithmetic and screen 53 will catch it. If they do not,
their mistake was here, and they have just seen it.

### 4.5 — *"Now calculate."*  (screen 53)

The substitution locks above, with the differences done:

```
d = √((6)² + (−8)²)
```

and the lock comes up. **Distance = [ ] units.**

- **Wrong once**: *"6 squared is 36. 8 squared is 64. What is the square
  root of 100?"* — which gives them everything except the last step,
  because the last step is the only one they still have to take.
- **Wrong twice**: the working writes itself out on the paper, the way
  every other spent ladder in this game does.

Then straight on to the rescue. **The towers are not asked again.**

## 5. The slider  *(screen 54)*

### 5.1 Why it earns its place

The lock is a control for *digits* — you set tens and units, and the
answer feels like a combination. A slider is a control for *magnitude*:
you push a value along a number line and watch it approach. For a
question that means "how far is it?", a slider **is a ruler**, and that
is a better instrument for this question than a keypad.

It also buys the one habit this whole topic needs: a child who can see
the line is a bit longer than 12 has a check on their own arithmetic.
The slider is where estimation and calculation meet.

### 5.2 What it must not become

A slider you can drag until the line lands on the vehicle is a
dexterity game, and the formula never gets used. So:

- **Nothing moves on the board while the slider moves.** Before the
  first Check, the slider is a number and the board is a picture.
- **After the first miss, the ruler appears**: from then on, the slider
  lays a line out of the control station along the true bearing, as long
  as the number it is showing. Short of the vehicle, past it, or exactly
  on it. The scaffold arrives when it is needed, which is how everything
  else in this game works — and by then they have already had their
  ungiven go.

### 5.3 How it behaves

- **0 to 15**, whole numbers only. A slider that lands on 12.7 makes
  Check a lottery.
- The current value large beside it — **13 units** — as the mock shows.
- Ticks at every unit, numbers at 0, 5, 10, 15. Marked in the board's
  own units, so the track and the grid can be compared by eye.
- **A Check button**, and no verdict until it is pressed.
- **Arrow keys move it and the track is tappable.** A control you can
  only answer by dragging is a control some children cannot answer at
  all. The value is announced when it changes.
- The range must reach the answer and leave room to overshoot, and must
  not offer a number the board cannot draw a line to — the rule
  `qa-range` enforces. 0..15 against an answer of 13 satisfies both.

## 6. The wide board  *(and the one thing the mocks got wrong)*

The board is **x −6..6, y −5..5** everywhere in this game. `(−5, −12)`
does not fit on it, and this is the largest piece of work in the
request.

**The mock's own grid is the wrong answer to it.** Read it closely: its
squares are worth about two and a half units each, so the vehicle at
`(−5, −12)` lands **between gridlines** — four and four-fifths of a
square down. A point that does not sit on an intersection cannot be
counted to, and counting off the board is this entire lesson's method.
Whatever else changes, that must not.

**So make the cells smaller, not the units bigger.** One unit stays one
cell; the screen picks a range:

| board | x | y | numbered every | cell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `close` (today, default) | −7..7 | −6..6 | 1 | 77 × 78 |
| `wide` (new) | −17..17 | −15..15 | 5 | ~35 × 35 |

*(Built as x −15..15, y −13..13 numbered, ruled −17..17 × −15..15: a
symmetric range is height-limited on a board wider than it is tall, the
same reason `close` stops at ±6/±5. See §11.2.)*

At `wide`, `(−5, −12)` is five cells left and twelve cells down, exactly
on an intersection, with three rows below it for its label and its
pill. Every point is still countable; there are just more, smaller
squares, and only every fifth line is numbered so the axis does not
become a wall of digits.

Everything on the board already positions itself as `origin + n × step`,
so the rest follows from `stepX`/`stepY`. Two things do not and must be
handled: `Board.build()` bakes the axis numbering once, so it has to be
able to rebuild when the range changes; and `GRID.paper` has to scale
with it, or every clamp in the game will be measuring against the wrong
sheet.

## 7. The rescue  *(screen 54, continued)*

**The leaves sweep.** The board comes back wide, and a new pair is on it:

| | where | |
|---|---|---|
| Control Station | **(0, 0)** | on the origin, which is the point |
| Rescue Vehicle | **(−5, −12)** | third quadrant |

Joined by a dashed line, both labelled with their coordinates, exactly
as every pair in this game is.

**Her lines**, two:

> *"The station is right at zero."* … *"How far is the rescue vehicle
> from it?"*

The first is the teaching and it is four words long. It says the thing
that makes this pair different before the question that makes it matter,
which is the order those two belong in.

**The hint**, one rung:

> *"One of them is zero. What is left to take away?"*

Not the mock's *"Find the distance from (0, 0)"*, which is the question
with the answer's starting point handed over.

**The ladder:**

- **Wrong once**: *"Nothing to subtract from zero — it is just 5 and
  12."* And the ruler appears under the slider (§5.2).
- **Wrong twice**: the working writes itself on the paper —
  `d = √(5² + 12²)` → `√(25 + 144)` → `√169 = 13` — with each part
  lighting the leg it names, as every working in this game does.

**And then it ends.** One line over the finished board:

> *"From zero, the coordinates are the distance."*

That sentence is what these six screens were for. It is the only thing
in the lesson that is true of *every* point on the plane without a
subtraction, and it is the right last thing for a child to be holding.

## 8. How to build it

1. **Six screens at the end of `SCRIPT`**: 49 the towers, 50–53 the
   repair, 54 the rescue. Screen 48 stops being the last beat; its line
   is unchanged, it just is not the ending any more.
2. **The branch already exists.** `task.rightAt` steps a correct answer
   over the teaching and `task.teachAt` sends a spent ladder into it —
   both built for the café beat. They are on the **choice** path only;
   the towers are a numeric question, so `answerRight` and the entry
   ladder need the same two hooks. That is the whole of the flow
   control: 49 right → 54, 49 spent → 50.
3. **The substitution panel is new** — `js/formula-slots.js` and its
   css. Four slots, four colour-coded chips, tap-or-drag, both orders
   accepted, self-filling on a spent ladder. Nothing else in the game
   does this and nothing else should have to know about it.
4. **The slider is new** — `js/distance-slider.js` and its css — but it
   answers the **existing numeric task**, so `task.kind` does not
   change; a screen only says which control it wants. Keyboard, tappable
   track, integer steps, announced value.
5. **The wide board** is §6: a named range on `GRID`, a screen that can
   pick one, `build()` able to renumber, and `paper` scaling with it.
6. **The ruler under the slider** reuses the measuring line the lock
   already lays on a distance question — pointed along the pair's own
   bearing rather than along a row.
7. **Every spoken line in config**, every duration in config, and
   everything through the game's own `later()` — never a bare
   `setTimeout`.

## 9. What must not break

**The teaching**

- **The towers beat helps with nothing** until it is answered wrong.
- **The repair diagnoses before it fixes.** Substitution comes before
  calculation, always, and the child fills it.
- **Both substitution orders are accepted.**
- **Nothing is asked a third time** — not the towers, not the
  substitution, not the calculation.
- **Nothing moves on the board while the slider moves**, until the first
  miss has been made.
- **Every point sits on a gridline**, on both boards.
- The rescue beat says why zero is different, and says it before the
  question.

**The build**

- **Every screen before 49 is untouched**, on the `close` board they
  have always used.
- A right answer at 49 never shows the repair; a wrong one never returns
  to the towers.
- Back and Next replay each of the six cleanly, from either direction,
  with no chip, no slot, no ruler and no control left behind.
- The picker can land on any of the six and each draws its own pair —
  the rule `qa-picked` enforces.
- The slider is answerable without a mouse.
- A square stays square on the wide board.
- The game still ends after 54.

### 11.2 What the spec did not anticipate

**The substitution line does not fit the column, and forcing it was the
wrong answer.** §4.4 asks for `d = √((▢ − ▢)² + (▢ − ▢)²)` with four
tappable slots inside 520px. At any type size where the slots are big
enough for a child to hit, that line is wider than her column. What is
built is the **squared form** — `d² = (□ − □)² + (□ − □)²` — which is
the form the rest of this lesson already writes: every working on the
board says `AB² = 4² + 3²` and then takes the root. The root is screen
53's job and is not asked for twice; leaving it out is what lets the
slots be 58px instead of 40.

**A negative lands in a slot without brackets round it** — `(4 − −2)²`.
`one-more-walk.md` §6 is firm that a bracket is what makes a
minus-a-minus visible instead of a typo, and it is right about a written
line. Here the slot's own box does that job: a boxed `−2` is
unmistakably one value, and it carries its tower's colour underneath.

**The wide board is not quite §6's table.** A symmetric ±15 is
height-limited on a board that is wider than it is tall, exactly as the
close board's ±6/±5 is:

| | numbered | ruled | cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| `close` | x −6..6, y −5..5, every 1 | −7..7 × −6..6 | 88 × 88 |
| `wide` | x −15..15, y −13..13, every 5 | −17..17 × −15..15 | 35.2 × 35.2 |

`wide` is `close` at two fifths — both steps scaled by the same factor,
so the cells stay square — and the origin does not move, because both
ranges are centred on the viewBox. `(−5, −12)` is five cells left and
twelve down, **exactly on an intersection**, with three rows below it
for its label. That was the whole point of §6 and it holds.

**The origin had to be told where to put its own labels.** No point in
this game had ever sat on `(0, 0)`. Below it is the x numbering and to
its left is the y numbering, and every placement rule the board has is
about getting clear of the *other point*. A point can now carry its own
`coordDx`/`coordDy` in cells: the station's coordinate goes up and right
of the axis, its letter up and left, so the two are on opposite sides of
it rather than stacked on it.

**Screen 51 needed the formula actually drawn.** The spec says it
"arrives beside the board" and the first build said the line and drew
nothing. It uses `derive` — the "state a derivation" hook from
`one-question-better.md` §9.2 — with a single line, so the board comes
to the middle, pushes in and writes the formula on the paper next to
the two towers.

### 11.3 The expensive one: `.full`

A filled slot marked itself `full`. `css/style.css` has, for the
full-frame artwork:

```css
.full { position: absolute; left: 0; top: 0; width: 1920px; height: 1080px; }
```

So every slot that took a number became a screen-sized layer pinned to
the panel's corner. The symptom was a row of algebra that laid out
correctly while empty and collapsed into a heap the moment a chip
landed — and it survived a rewrite of that row in flex, in inline-block
and in grid, because none of those was the problem. Nothing about it
pointed at the class name.

**`qa-classes` is new and guards it**: no class a component writes may
be one the game styles with a bare `.name` selector. Shared state words
(`hidden`, `on`, `pop`, `rising`, `wait`, `now`, `draw`, `lit`) are
allowed by name, because every rule that paints them is scoped to the
panel it belongs to. Reverting the fix turns the probe red.

### 11.4 Four more found while verifying

1. **Neither new component attached itself.** Both built their markup
   and returned a handle without ever appending it to the scene. The
   Node harness reaches a panel through its own handle, so every probe
   passed; nothing was on the frame.
2. **An entrance animation was never taken off.** `rising` is filled
   (`both`), so while the class is on, the animation's last frame *is*
   the panel's transform and the base rule can never apply. Both
   components now drop it on `animationend`, with a timer behind it.
3. **Keeping a control meant keeping *a* control, not *the* control.**
   Four screens in a row change control between them, and 53 — which
   inherits from the substitution panel — came up with no control at
   all. `keepsControl` now compares what this screen wants with what she
   is standing on.
4. **53's nudge was a lecture.** Three sentences, and the balloon was
   still going twenty seconds later. It is one sentence now — *"Square
   them, add, then take the root."* — which still gives everything
   except the last step.

## 12. The lock, put back on its own art — 19 Sep 2026

Not part of the six beats, but it is the control two of them use.

**The arrows were each a whole `--frame-x` to the right of their
socket.** The art bulges a round lobe out of the housing at each end
for them to sit in; the left arrow was sitting inside the housing and
the right one was hanging past its own bulge with the cream lobe
showing between. The cause is one line of markup: the arrows are
children of the **frame**, and every offset was written as though they
were children of the **widget**, so each picked up the frame's own
inset a second time. GO looked perfect throughout — GO really is the
widget's child.

Centred now by folding the half-width into `left` and `top` rather than
by a negative margin, because `:hover` and `:active` both put a
transform on these and a centring transform would fight them. The
widget also reserves the width it actually needs — `--frame-x` is half
an arrow plus a little air, and `pos.x` is pulled back by the same
amount, so the housing has not moved a pixel.

**And the three barrels were one shape.** The sheet has two drawings —
the chosen barrel is 155 × 182 and a plain one is 144 × 155, so the gold
one is both wider and a good deal taller — and both were being stretched
into the same box, which drew three identical drums. Each is sized from
its own drawing now: the gold one fills the slot top to bottom and the
two beside it sit short of it, which is what makes the chosen one read
as chosen before the colour does. The plain digits are set to their own
barrel rather than to the gold one's, so all three look like one row of
type.

The window's own fractions are re-measured off the sheet while the
frame was open: `y 0.2283, h 0.3762` against the `0.2251 / 0.3891` that
were there.

**`qa-lockfit`** is new: which parent each piece has and therefore which
offsets it may use, both barrel ratios against the actual PNG headers,
and the widget being wide enough for its own overhang. Suite is at
**91 green, 4 red** — the standing four.
